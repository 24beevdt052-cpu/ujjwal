Read the third and fourth columns together. In March the base screen pays four times what the
point-in-time mix does (T03: ₹43,205 against ₹10,406), which says most of that margin is the lag-2 grade
reconstruction rather than something a 2022 desk could have banked — and the tickets written in those
weeks are correspondingly small. By late May the two screens cross over and the point-in-time number
becomes the friendly one (T07: ₹39,374 against ₹22,935). The desk sizes off whichever is smaller.

**The three screens are not three independent screens, and this page used to imply they were.** Over the
window's 162 week × grade × lane cases the base screen is open 111 times, the
point-in-time-mix screen 152 times and the conversion-stressed screen 82 times — and
`trade_eligible` equals `open_conv18k` on all 258 rows of `parity_weekly.csv`, not just in
the window. The point-in-time screen excludes **0** cases that the stressed screen
does not already exclude, while the stressed screen shuts 70 cases the
point-in-time screen would have left open. Both the base and the stressed screen run on the same lag-2
reconstructed grade mix, so **the binding gate is a hindsight-mix screen** and the no-hindsight screen
never binds. Two consequences a reader is entitled to:

1. The book's most visible piece of discipline — standing aside for the 5 consecutive
   weeks from 2022-06-17 to 2022-07-15, the last leg of the crash — was produced by a screen a
   2022 desk could not have computed. On every one of those weeks the point-in-time screen was open on at
   least 6 of the 6 grade × lane cases. A desk actually trading the
   point-in-time screen would have kept buying into the low.
2. Entry *timing* in this book is therefore not point-in-time, even though every ticket's price, factor,
   fixture and hedge size is re-derived from data dated on or before its own day. The §5a rule was
   declared before Phase 1 ran and may not be changed after seeing results, so the rule stays and the
   claim about it changes. Phase 1 owns the honest alternative gate and what it would have cost
   (`docs/10_parity_model.md` §5a).


## 4. The desk policy this book was written to

Stated before the book was priced, applied mechanically, and re-derived point-in-time by
`desk.book.validate` so it cannot be quietly abandoned. None of it is fitted to an outcome; where a
ticket departs from it, the ticket says why on its trade date.

| # | Policy | Value | Enforced by |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | Hedge the **net LME-equivalent delta**, not the tonnage. Unsold cargo is long `grade factor × k`; a contracted MCX-average sale is long `factor × duty-paid parity`; a fixed-rupee sale is flat; a purchase still inside its LME quotational period is short `factor × k`. `k` is the ₹/t move of a CFR cargo's landed value per USD/t of LME. | — | P07 |
| H2 | Ratio **0.90** on a fixed-price cargo with no contracted sale (a naked long); **1.00** on the residual between two floating legs; one ticket at **0.50** by exception. | 0.90 / 1.00 / 0.50 | P07 |
| H3 | Size in whole 5 MT lots: `lots = round(ratio × net ₹-delta ÷ ₹-delta of one lot)`. A **roll keeps the position** it continues; the desk re-sizes only at a new decision. | — | P06, P07 |
| H4 | Roll **7 trading days before the DIRECT expiry**, never later: two days' cushion before the delivery tender period and the 25 % delivery-period margin. Positions are keyed by contract **month**, so the panel's 31-Aug slot and the exchange's 30-Aug expiry are the same contract. | 7 trading days | P06 |
| H5 | An **averaging leg** is treated as open until the ceil(n/2)-th trading day of its window and closed after it, so a hedge against a formula leg is lifted once, at the midpoint, rather than in n pieces. | midpoint day | P07 |
| H6 | Cover **100 % of the contracted USD payable** with deliverable forwards matched to the payment date, **dealt on the bill of lading** — the first day on which both the amount and the payment date exist. The desk is therefore uncovered in USD between the trade date and the first B/L and says so; on a formula purchase only the provisional invoice is coverable at the B/L, and the final balance waits for the quotational month to close. One ticket covers 60% by exception. | 1.00 (T06 0.60) | schema V18 |
| F1 | **Freight is not hedged.** No accessible India-lane container derivative existed in 2022, so an FOB cargo floats under a written stop-loss at the trade-date lane index **+ 15%**, with a hard book-by date 10 days before the first B/L. | +15 %, book-by B/L −10d | P05 |
| F2 | The NVOCC all-in rate is expected to sit **0%–5% over the assessed lane index** of the fixture date. The spread itself is an ASSUMPTION — no 2022 India-inbound fixture evidence exists in the cached sources. | 0–5 % | P05 |
| S1 | A domestic sale is priced at **replacement value + 50% × (smelter netback − replacement value)** on the contract date, then adjusted for payment terms at the desk's own `wc_rate_inr_pa` — an advance is worth `price × wc × 30 days × advance fraction`, credit past 30 days costs `price × wc × extra days` — and rounded to the nearest ₹250/MT. Both sides are the same arithmetic; the first draft paid a flat ₹500/MT for an advance and charged ₹500/MT a fortnight for credit, and both errors ran the desk's way. | 50 % of the gap | P09 |
| S2 | The desk bids **0–30 USD/t under the trade-date parity price** on the incoterm basis; a formula purchase is contracted 0–1.0 point under the trade-date grade factor. | 0–30 USD/t | P10 |

| C2 | An **advance the desk relies on** is performance exposure, not credit, and is capped at `buyer_advance_limit_multiple_of_credit_limit` x that buyer's own limit. This book breaches it three times, deliberately and on the record (§2). | 1.00x | P14 |

**Why a 50/50 split on the sale.** Phase 1's net arbitrage is dominated by `domestic_anchor_premium_inr_t`,
an ASSUMPTION whose own evidence spans −₹52k to +₹13k/t. Letting the desk take the whole modelled
arbitrage would put ₹30,000/MT of March margin on the book on the strength of one assumption. Splitting
it in half is the conservative reading and the one a competitive import market would produce; it is a
stated convention, not an observation, and it is the single largest judgement on this page.

**And here is what that judgement is worth, because defending it is not enough.** Nobody ever quoted this
desk a price. Both sides of every trade are set by the desk's own two rules — S2 against its own parity
on the buy, S1 against its own replacement-and-netback bounds on the sell — so the day-one margin below
is an arithmetic property of those rules and not the outcome of a negotiation. The same nine tickets,
repriced at other conventions with nothing else changed:

| Sale priced at | Day-one sale margin ₹ |
|---|---|
| 50% of the modelled arbitrage  ← the book | 172.7 mn |
| 25% of the modelled arbitrage | 83.8 mn |
| 10% of the modelled arbitrage | 30.5 mn |
| 0% of the modelled arbitrage | -5.1 mn |

Day-one sale margin is Σ tonnes × (contracted sale price − import replacement value on the contract date):
what the book books the moment a sale is signed, before any market move. Read it as the width of one
judgement, not as a P&L. At a 25 % share the book keeps under half of it; at a flat ₹5,000–8,000/MT —
which is what a competitive import market actually pays a middleman — two fifths to two thirds; at 0 % the
desk is a logistics provider working for its payment terms. The purchase side is narrower and points the
same way: rule S2 allows 0–30 USD/t under parity and all six fixed-price tickets sit at 10–15 USD/t, the
top third of the desk's own band, across six independent 'negotiations' with three suppliers over five
months. Phase 3 should be read with this table beside it.


## 5. Ticket cards

Every Table 4 field for each trade, with the trader's rationale as written on the trade date. Dates in
a rationale are checked by `desk.book.validate` (code P08): a rationale may cite a market fact only with
a date on or before its own trade date, and a forward reference is allowed only where the ticket itself
declares that period as a contract term. The numbers in it are checked too (P13, §9).

**Read the Incoterm line carefully, because it says two different things.** Under **both** FOB and CFR
(Incoterms 2020 A2/B2) the risk of loss or damage passes to the buyer when the goods are **on board at
the load port**: CFR moves the *cost* of carriage to the seller, never the risk, and a CFR cargo that
sinks mid-ocean is the desk's cargo. What the incoterm changes for this desk is who carries the freight
*price* risk — the desk on FOB (it books the boxes, §6.4), the seller on CFR (freight is inside the price
and a CFR-basis grade factor does not move when the lane does). An earlier draft of this page said risk
sat with the seller until discharge on CFR, which is simply wrong, and the card now separates the two.

**And on using FOB/CFR for containers at all.** ICC's own guidance recommends FCA/CPT/CIP for
containerised cargo, precisely because "on board" does not describe a CY or terminal hand-over: the
seller loses control of the box at the gate but keeps the risk until it is loaded, days later. This book
uses FOB and CFR anyway, for one reason: that is what the non-ferrous scrap trade actually does — LC
presentation is built around an on-board bill of lading, and a scrap SPA quoting CFR India is the market
convention the grade factors in CONTRACTS §5 are quoted on. It is a deliberate departure from the ICC's
preference, not an oversight, and the honest version of the answer is that the desk accepts a gate-to-
loading gap it does not control on its three FOB tickets.

₹293.5409/kg, which is **+₹1,296,150** on the hedge. A genuinely limit-locked contract would have gapped down on the 9th
to catch up the unprinted move and the same delay would have cost money. The ticket says so.

Second, the **roll is a gain by construction in this book and that is an artefact, not a result**. The
panel builds MCX M1 and M2 from a single spot through a deterministic interest carry, so the modelled
curve is in permanent contango: all **13 rolls** in the book are gains, **₹8,421,610** in
total, with no loss anywhere. A short that rolls in contango does collect the spread — but the LME's own
curve went into backwardation from 20-Jul-2022 (`lme_cash_3m_spread_usd_t` turns positive, DIRECT), and a
real duty-paid parity curve carrying that slope would have made the same roll pay for its metal carry
(Phase 3 prices it: `mcx_roll_carry.csv`, `lme_curve_*` columns). Where a ticket calls the roll a *cost* of holding cargo (T07), that is the state of the
world the proxy cannot show, and both the ticket and this page now say so. Phase 3's roll bucket should be
read as the proxy's carry, not as evidence that rolling a short is free.

Third, a roll keeps the size of the line it continues, so the realised ratio drifts between decisions —
that drift is visible in the `hedge_ratio_actual` column and is deliberate.


### 6.2 Cross-exchange basis risk (Table 4 row 2.7c)

The desk owns LME-linked aluminium **scrap** and hedges it with MCX Aluminium, a **primary-ingot** contract
that trades at duty-paid import parity. Four mismatches survive the hedge, and each of them is a bucket in
the Phase 3 attribution rather than something the ratio can fix:

1. **The grade spread.** The hedge covers `grade_factor × LME`, not the grade factor itself. Zorba's
   factor rose from 0.699 on 08-Mar to 0.792 by 10-May because yards refused to follow the exchange down;
   a hedged cargo therefore *gained* on the discount while its flat price was neutralised. That is the
   grade-spread bucket (c), it is large in this book, and it rests on a reconstructed lag-2 unit-value
   ratio — so it is a reconstruction, not an observed market move.
2. **Timing.** LME Official Cash is fixed at the London midday ring; MCX settles in the Indian evening.
   The two are not the same moment and the panel runs MCX on the LME calendar (CONTRACTS §3).
3. **The duty base.** The physical cargo's duty runs on the CBIC notified rate, frozen for a fortnight;
   MCX's parity uplift runs on the market rate. A short MCX position is also short USD, so it loses when
   the rupee weakens — visible in bucket (e), not (b).
4. **Parity itself can break.** It visibly did in the first week of March 2022.

**The honest caveat.** In base runs the panel MCX series *is* the duty-paid parity formula, so the measured
cross-exchange basis is **zero by construction** — the (b) bucket will be empty and that is an artefact of
the proxy, not evidence that the basis is small. The only basis evidence in this project is the
third-party mirror sensitivity (CONTRACTS §7.5) and MCX's own published figure: a basis standard deviation
of 4.44 ₹/kg for FY2022-23, on a same-product hedge. A
cross-product scrap-versus-primary hedge is weaker still.

**When each forward is dealt, and why it moved.** Every purchase-matched forward is booked **on the bill

payable from the trade date to the first B/L** (13 to 21 days on the fixed-price tickets), and that

certainty actually uses — cover at contract to an estimated date, then pay swap points to realign when the
vessel moves — is NOT used here, because this project does not model swap points and a hedge whose
realignment is free is worth more than a hedge. Say which one you are looking at before comparing.

balance has become a supplier refund, T05 does the same on 01-Jul after June closes, and T08 buys its
balance forward on 01-Aug. (T05 previously carried a USD 200,000 **BUY** dealt at inception against an
expected balance and cancelled when none appeared. That was not a hedge — it was a position on an unknown
number, and it was removed in review; see §12.) T06
covers only 60% of its payable on
purpose: the cargo had no rupee revenue contracted yet, and a fully covered payable against an
uncontracted sale is a view, not a hedge. The rupee then went from 77.26 to over 80, so that decision
cost money — which is the point of writing it down beforehand.

### 6.5 Schedule risk — the exposure this book does not carry (Table 4 row 2.8)

Nineteen sailings, twenty bills of lading, and until this review **not one day of schedule slippage**
anywhere in the book — in the worst year for container schedule reliability on record. Two lots now carry
the void-call arrival delay (T08, §7), which is the honest channel for that event, but every other B/L is
still laycan-start + 2 days and every transit is still exactly the registered 5 or 40 days. That is a
modelling convenience, and on the three M+1 tickets it is load-bearing rather than cosmetic: each asserts
that its laycan sits inside one calendar month, **so the quotational period is fixed at signature**. If a
bill of lading slips into the next month, the period moves with it and the purchase reprices against a
different month's average:

### 6.6 What went right that the desk did not choose

The two deliberately sub-optimal calls this page puts on the record — T04's 0.50 MCX ratio and T06's 60 %
dollar cover — are advertised, and both tickets still finish profitable in Phase 3. A book that confesses
only its small mistakes deserves to be discounted, so here are the unflagged judgement calls that paid,
each measured on this page's own numbers:

| Call nobody flagged | What happened | Worth |
|---|---|---|
| T01's hedge deferred one session on a limit-lock inferred from the **proxy** (§6.1) | the proxy had already printed the move, so the short went on higher | +₹1,296,150 |
| All three FOB fixtures left open to the book-by date (§6.4) | the lane index fell | USD 15,066 (≈ ₹1.2 mn) |
| The stop-loss never tested (§6.4) | freight never rose; had each FOB index gone straight to its stop, the same policy would have cost | −USD 72,534 (≈ −₹5.6 mn) |
| No M+1 lot slipped a month (§6.5) | the market fell, so two of the three slips would have *helped*; the risk was symmetric and simply not drawn | −₹1.5 mn to +₹9.4 mn per lot |

The stop row is the counterfactual the book never exercises: a +15 % stop still lets the freight layer
lose about 5 times what waiting gained before it binds, so the layer's 2022 result is the market's direction, not the policy's
protection. The last row is the largest by far and the least earned: it is the reconstructed grade path,
not a decision.

The **logistics trigger is real and dated**: Container News, 27-Jul-2022, reported carriers voiding calls
at Nhava Sheva and Mundra (`docs/research/freight_notes.md` S4). The schema refuses any ticket that claims
to know about it earlier. The days attributed to it are simulated. **The channel matters and the first
draft had it wrong**: a voided call is an ocean-side event, so its first-order effect is a rolled or
omitted box — an arrival delay, not yard rent. That is now modelled where it can be, on T08, whose two
lots were still at sea on 27-Jul (undelayed ETAs 01-Aug and 08-Aug). T07's two lots had already landed and
cleared their Bills of Entry before the news broke, so what they carry is the plausible *second-order*
landside consequence — a congested yard and slow evacuation once the omitted boxes come back through the
same terminal — and both the ticket and this table now say which is which.

**The radiation-portal box is not free, and it used to be.** An alarmed container at Mundra does not
quietly leave the Bill of Entry while the rest of the consignment clears: it is referred to Customs and
the AERB, the BoE is held, and the other 39 boxes accrue detention until the referral closes. That is
event T08-LOG-3 — 14 simulated days, taking the lot to 24 days against 14 free, so 10 chargeable days on
39 boxes. The alarmed box itself is then held for `rejected_box_hold_days` (45, ASSUMPTION) accruing slabbed detention and re-exported at
`rejected_box_reexport_cost_usd_per_box` (USD 3,000, ASSUMPTION: return freight, handling, a radiological survey and the
permissions) — paid by the desk first and claimed back from the seller at the event's recovery. The first
draft modelled none of that, so the event used to be free. Decontamination or disposal of a genuinely
contaminated box is **still not modelled** and can cost far more; the book assumes the common case, a
re-exportable source. Both quality events carry `claim_recovery_frac` **0.6**, not the 1.0 of the first
draft: after an LC has paid at sight, a claim on the smallest yard on the register is an unsecured
negotiation, and a desk that recovers 100 % of every claim is a desk that is indifferent to out-turn — the
opposite of why the clause exists. On the formula-priced T08 the goods deduction is set off against the final
invoice, which secures the cash but not the claim, so the same 0.6 applies. Phase 3 publishes the book at
0.0 and 1.0 beside it (`pnl_sensitivity_summary.csv`, `claim_recovery_*`).

**Detention is slabbed.** `demurrage_usd_per_box_day` is the FIRST-slab rate and its own register note
says carriers escalate after five to ten days, so charging an eight- or ten-day delay at it understates
the bill. `desk.book.validate.demurrage_usd` now walks the registered slabs
(`demurrage_slab1_days`, `demurrage_slab2_days`, `demurrage_usd_per_box_day_slab2/3`) and
`trade_book.csv` publishes both figures side by side. The Phase 3 engine uses the same schedule
(`desk.mtm.lifecycle.detention_usd`, asserted equal in `tests/test_mtm_engine.py`). The four keys price
carrier detention and CFS ground rent **combined**; they are not split into separate tariffs with separate
free periods, so the slabbed figure is still an estimate of the order of the bill, not a line tariff.

The **buyer payment delay is entirely simulated** and is placed on the weakest name in the book, a Rajkot
foundry that already pays 70 % of that parcel in advance — so the delay hits the balance rather than the
whole cargo, which is what the credit policy is for. Read it with §2: the advance that keeps the
receivable small is itself 1.8× that buyer's credit limit.
The **quality outcomes are simulated** but sit on the SPA's own registered clauses: T07's lot is 1.4
points over the moisture franchise and 1.1 points over the contamination limit (a weight deduction plus a
1.65 % price discount, inside the 3-point rejection threshold).

The book passes with **0 errors and 3 warnings**, and the warnings are not noise to be cleared:
all three are P14, the advance-reliance cap, on the
three BUY_RJK_01 parcels (§2). A control that has never fired on the real book is a control nobody has
tested; a control that fires and is then argued with in writing is the point.

**What P13 can and cannot check.** It reconciles the shapes a desk actually writes — "N dollars in a
day", "N dollars off the D-Mon low", "N dollars under my netback", "rupee at XX.XX", "moved N % today"
— against the panel on the decision date or on the parity week the ticket cites, and it refuses the
categorical claim that a ticket's inputs were all knowable on the day. It does **not** understand prose:
a claim it cannot parse is not checked and is not counted as passing. It caught one wrong number in this
book (T03's bounce off the 15-Mar low, typed as 180 dollars against a panel move of 306) and one
unsupportable provenance claim (T01's "every input is dated 08-Mar-2022 or earlier"); both are fixed in
the tickets, not in the scanner.

`tests/test_book.py` runs a mutation table: one deliberately broken copy of this book per
code, asserting the code fires and the message names the thing that is wrong. A validation that has never
been shown to fail is not a control.


## 10. What this does and doesn't tell you

**Does.** It turns Phase 1's weekly parity screen into nine dated trading decisions with every commercial
term a physical desk actually negotiates: an ISRI grade specification with a moisture franchise and a
contamination discount schedule, an incoterm that says who books and bears the freight, a pricing formula
with an explicit quotational period, a payment instrument with a tenor, a laycan a real lane could serve,
a buyer with a credit limit that sometimes binds, and a hedge stack sized by a written rule. It shows the
hedging decisions a scrap importer can actually take in India — MCX Aluminium and deliverable USD/INR
forwards — and it is explicit that freight is not one of them. Every decision is checked against data
dated on or before it, mechanically, by code that raises.

**Doesn't.** It is not evidence of what any desk earned in 2022, and it is not a backtest of a strategy.
The trades are chosen by one person with a screen in front of them, and a different trader with the same
screen would have written a different book. The counterparties, vessels, survey outcomes and the payment
delay are invented. The sale prices are not quotes: they are a stated 50/50 split of a modelled
arbitrage whose size is dominated by `domestic_anchor_premium_inr_t`, an assumption with an evidence range
of ₹65,000/t — so the book's headline margins are as uncertain as that one number, and a reader who wants
one figure to distrust should distrust that one. Grade factors are a hindsight-calibrated reconstruction
published months after the fact, so the grade-spread result is a property of the reconstruction as much as
of the market. Freight levels are reconstructed too. MCX is a duty-parity proxy, which means the hedge is
priced off the same LME the cargo is priced off and the cross-exchange basis — the risk this book is most
exposed to in reality — is invisible in base runs. And nothing here is P&L: this phase produces positions
and terms; Phase 3 marks them.

Four more things this book does not contain, each of which a physical desk would. There is **no
negotiation**: no counterparty ever holds out, and both legs of every trade come out of the desk's own two
pricing rules, which is why §4 publishes what the result looks like at other conventions. There is **no
schedule slippage** except the two void-call arrival delays on T08 — nineteen sailings in the worst year
on record for schedule reliability, and §6.5 sizes what one slipped bill of lading would have been worth.
There is **no funding or facility limit** anywhere: this phase produces positions, and the cash they
consume is Phase 3's to report, but no ticket was ever tested against a working-capital line. And the
**grade reconstruction sets both the buy and the sell price** — the purchase is struck against a
reconstructed parity and the sale against a replacement value built from the same factor — so it is not
only an attribution exposure that nets out in a re-mark; it is in the contracted numbers themselves.

**The one thing a reader should take at face value** is that every price, factor, fixture and hedge size
here was re-derived, mechanically, from data dated on or before the day it is typed against, by code that
raises. Not the trade *timing*: §3 shows the gate that produced it is a hindsight-mix screen, and this
page says so rather than selling the discipline it would like to have had.


## 11. Changes during integration with Phase 3

Phase 2 (this book) and Phase 3 (`desk/mtm`, the valuation engine) were built concurrently against
`docs/design/30_position_model.md`. When they were joined the engine loaded this book, priced it and
passed every sign-off control on the first run.

**No ticket was changed by the integration.** `config/trades.yaml` and `config/counterparties.yaml` came
through the join byte-for-byte: no schema fix, no date reformatting, no missing technical field, and no
economic correction. Nothing in the nine tickets broke eligibility, the horizon, the container arithmetic
or a hedge-sizing rule when a second implementation valued them. That is the result of this heading, and
it is reported because an empty change log is only credible if someone says it is empty on purpose. The
independent review that came after integration is a different matter and is logged in §12.

One **apparent** contradiction between the phases was resolved, on the Phase 3 side:

- Phase 3's event-3 table first reported a buyer credit-limit breach of 2.58x over 46 days, against the
  peak utilisations of 78-93% this page reports. Neither book was wrong, and neither number is the other's
  answer: **they are two different measures and this paragraph used to conflate them.** Rule P04 here
  measures, at every sale booking, the receivable plus the uncovered part of sales already contracted —
  a booking-time control, and its peak is the 93% (BUY_MUN_01) in §2. Phase 3 measures the receivable
  *daily*, per CONTRACTS §7a.4, and its peak is a different name on a different day; it publishes that
  figure in `outputs/tables/adverse_event_3_logistics_credit.csv`, and this page does not restate it.
  Two things follow that a reader should have. The P2 measure evaluated daily rather than at bookings
  would exceed 100% on two names, because it counts contracted-but-uninvoiced sales; and neither measure
  sees the advances, which is the exposure §2 and rule P14 exist for. "The two phases agree" was the
  wrong claim: they measure different things, each cleanly, and both are published.

The engine's own two fixes (a final invoice that could precede its survey; an event known later than the
milestone it moves) were defects in the *engine*, not in these tickets, and are recorded in
`docs/30_mtm_attribution.md` §11.3.

**What was deliberately NOT changed.** The 50/50 arbitrage split stays — it was declared before the book
was priced and re-fitting it after seeing results would be worse than disclosing it, so §4 publishes the
sensitivity instead. The §5a gate stays, for the same reason, and §3 restates what it actually is. No
trade was added, removed or re-dated, and no hedge was re-sized.

**What this phase handed on, and where it landed.** Four things were outside this phase's code and
were handed to the Phase 3 engine; all four are now done there: (a) the slabbed detention schedule
(`desk.mtm.lifecycle.detention_usd`, asserted equal to `desk.book.validate.demurrage_usd`); (b) a typed
`known_date` on a quality event beats the derived survey date; (c) `quantity_tolerance_frac` is tested
with `is not None`, so a strict-quantity SPA no longer inherits the 10 % LC tolerance; and (d) per-buyer
exposure is published at (date, trade, buyer) grain in `buyer_credit_exposure_by_trade_daily.csv`, beside
the trade-grain file whose compound `buyer_id` no credit tracker could group.

**What the review found that no ticket change can fix.** Both bounds of rule S1 — the replacement mark
and the smelter netback — are the desk's own reconstructions (lag-2 grade factors, an anchor premium
nobody observed in 2022), so the day-one margin in §4 is conditional on them. Phase 3 re-prices every
ticket through S1/S2 under each registered band (`docs/30_mtm_attribution.md` §13.9): the book's sign
does **not** survive the bottom of the `domestic_anchor_premium_sensitivity_inr_t` grid. That result is
reported there, and this page should not be quoted without it.
